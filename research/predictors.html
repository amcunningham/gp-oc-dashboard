What predicts patient experience?

The models behind the practice lookup. Practice-level analysis of English practices; full analysis notes in PANEL_NOTES.md. Everything here is association across practices, not cause and effect.

The two outcomes, from the GP Patient Survey 2026 (fieldwork January–April 2026; practices with at least 30 responses):

Overall experience — Q32: "Overall, how would you describe your experience of your GP practice?" (% very or fairly good).

Most recent contact — Q16: "Overall, how would you describe your experience of contacting your GP practice on this occasion?" (% very or fairly good). This is about the last attempt to get in touch, not access in general.

Easy to contact on the phone — Q1: "Generally, how easy or difficult is it to contact your GP practice on the phone?" (% very or fairly easy). The phone-specific report, and the outcome the operational data should predict best.

How to read the tables. Every predictor except the training flag is standardised, so each figure is the difference in the outcome (percentage points) associated with being one standard deviation higher on that predictor; the training flag is the difference associated with hosting at least a quarter of a full-time GP registrar. "Alone" looks at one predictor at a time. "Together" puts the operational measures and the practice's circumstances in one model. "+ patients report" adds the three measures patients themselves report: seeing a preferred clinician (Q7, Q6) and being told to contact the practice again (Q12). Figures in grey are not statistically distinguishable from zero (p≥0.05).

Reading these tables

The largest coefficient in either model belongs to patients reporting they were told to contact the practice again another day (Q12). This measure is strongly tied to capacity — deprivation, unanswered calls and GP numbers predict a fifth of it (final section) — so a high figure partly reflects demand exceeding the appointments available, which is not quickly fixable. It is not fully determined by capacity, though:

Practices under the same measured pressure differ several-fold: a tenth of even the most pressured keep the figure at or below 3.4% — beneath the national median — while a tenth exceed 19%. What separates them is not measurable in these data; the survey does not record what patients are offered when no slot is left. The largest positive coefficient belongs to patients getting to see the clinician they prefer (Q7), which is substantially a matter of how appointments are allocated. Among the purely operational measures, answering the phone reliably matters most, and calls ending in the phone menus carry a separate unfavourable association. The Monday–Wednesday answering gap and contacts per appointment look important alone but stop mattering once the overall answer rate and calls per patient are in the model.

The phone-ease table behaves as an operational outcome should. The external data explains far more of it (R²=0.472) than of the experience outcomes (0.275/0.304), led by answering (+6.58) and IVR endings (−3.38). Three of its results deserve note. Practice size carries its largest disadvantage here (−4.82 with everything controlled) — larger practices' phones are harder to reach in ways the answering rate alone does not capture; call waiting times, unmeasured until the June 2026 telephony release, are the obvious missing channel. Heavier online intake goes with markedly worse reported phone ease (−4.46) even at the same answering rate. And training practices look strongly unfavourable alone (−4.09) but favourable with controls (+2.66): they tend to be large, and being large pulls their raw scores down. Once size is taken into account, training practices score higher than their circumstances would predict, on all three outcomes.

Deprivation works largely through being turned away. Alone, deprivation carries one of the biggest disadvantages in the table (−2.29 / −2.45). With the patient-reported measures in the model it shrinks to a fraction (−0.57 / −0.77): practices in deprived areas score worse substantially because more of their patients report being told to come back another day. Read cautiously, that relocates much of the deprivation gap from demography to the meeting of demand and capacity — which is bleaker as a statement about workload, and less bleak as a statement about what can be done.

The size disadvantage decomposes almost entirely. Alone, larger practices score much lower (−2.31 / −3.33). With continuity, deflection and the phone measures accounted for, a small fraction remains (−0.38 / −0.93). Most of what looked like a cost of being large is the loss of continuity, more patients turned away, and fewer calls answered — all of which are things practices do, not things they are.

Training practices score higher — robustly. Practices hosting a GP registrar score about 1.7 to 2.7 points higher in every combined model, on both questions, over and above their staffing and circumstances — one of the most stable results in these tables. Point for point of full-time equivalent, trainee GPs are associated with experience much as fully-qualified GPs are. The advantage is not carried by continuity — trainees rotate, and trainee staffing goes with slightly lower reported continuity — so training practices score higher despite that. The likeliest reading is that training accreditation marks organisational quality the other variables cannot see; the same association was reported from the 2012 survey by Ahluwalia and colleagues (BJGP 2014), when 29% of practices trained. Our flag is cruder: hosting at least a quarter of a full-time registrar in the March 2025 census (59% of practices), which misses accredited practices between placements.

The same-day share might seem counterintuitive. Alone it looks slightly unfavourable; with the patient-reported measures held constant it turns slightly favourable (+0.49 / +0.58). Booking models built around same-day access tend to cost continuity and turn more people away when the day fills; once those costs are accounted for separately, the configuration itself is not the problem.

Admin staffing might seem counterintuitive. Alone, more admin staff goes with higher scores (+0.97). The combined model asks a narrower question: of two practices the same on everything else measured — size, deprivation, GPs, nurses, appointments — does the one with more admin staff score higher? It scores slightly lower (−1.06, shrinking to −0.39 with the patient-reported measures in). We tested the obvious explanations. It is not phone workload: adding calls per patient to the model leaves the result unchanged. It is not reception quality: patients at practices with more admin staff rate their reception teams as more helpful, not less (Q4), and allowing for that barely moves it. So we do not know what this remaining association reflects — possibly workload we don't measure, differences in which roles get counted as admin, or the difficulty of dividing credit among staffing numbers that rise and fall together. It should not be read as admin staff lowering scores — and equally, the table gives no support for expecting more admin staff alone to raise them. Nor does admin staffing predict how well the phones are answered:

The pattern is flat, and stays flat comparing practices of similar size. Admin headcount includes many roles besides answering phones, so total admin staffing is a poor guide to how well a practice's phones are answered.

Cautions. These are practice-level associations, not causes, and not predictions for any one practice. The patient-reported measures (Q6, Q7, Q12) come from the same survey respondents as the outcomes, so part of their strength reflects the same voices answering both questions; the operational and workforce measures do not share this problem. Contacts per appointment, calls per 1,000 and appointments per 1,000 share ingredients and should be read as a family rather than separately. The training flag and trainee FTE are derived from the difference between all-GP and fully-qualified-GP FTE in the March 2025 census — a placement snapshot, not an accreditation register. Phone measures are from May 2026; survey fieldwork ran January–April 2026. Coefficients in grey have p≥0.05; with this many terms, isolated marginal results should be treated as unconfirmed. The main conclusions replicate in the full population at almost identical size — deflection, continuity, the training-practice result, the admin result and the same-day result are not artefacts of which practices have phone data. Practice size is more unfavourable here (−0.98 / −1.70) than in the tables above (−0.38 / −0.93): large practices answer fewer of their calls, and with no phone variable in this model to carry that fact, it shows up attached to size instead. Reading the two together: being large goes with lower scores because patients less often see their preferred clinician, are more often told to come back, and fewer calls get answered — and little else.

Do patients' reports measure the same things as the machines?

The R² of any model containing the patient-reported measures (0.540 / 0.621 with phones; 0.535 / 0.609 without) is roughly double that of the purely external model (0.275 / 0.304). Before trusting those higher figures, it is worth asking how much of them is patients describing real operations, and how much is the same respondents answering related questions in the same mood. Two checks help.

First: the phone data alone, predicting each patient-reported phone question.

That is convergent validity in both directions: it supports the telephony data and the survey at once. Reports of being unable to contact the practice at all are too rare at practice level to carry a signal (the median practice records 0.0%).

The verdict: the patient-reported measures carry real operational information — the survey is not simply echoing itself — but they also share respondents with the outcome questions, so some of their strength is shared mood, in a proportion these data cannot pin down. The 0.275 explained from purely external data is the more demanding benchmark, and the phone measures remain the strongest independently measured predictors of what patients report.

This is also why reported phone ease appears in these pages as an outcome (its own table above) and never as a predictor: it re-measures the same thing the phone systems measure, and a model containing both inverts the operational coefficients into noise — answering the phone comes out unfavourable, which is absurd. Q12 and Q7 earn their place as predictors because they describe events no machine records: being turned away at the desk, and getting the preferred clinician.
