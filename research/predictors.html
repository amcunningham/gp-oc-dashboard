What predicts patient experience?

The models behind the practice lookup. Practice-level analysis of English practices; full analysis notes in PANEL_NOTES.md. Everything here is association across practices, not cause and effect.

The two outcomes, from the GP Patient Survey 2026 (fieldwork January–April 2026; practices with at least 30 responses):

Overall experience — Q32: "Overall, how would you describe your experience of your GP practice?" (% very or fairly good).

Most recent contact — Q16: "Overall, how would you describe your experience of contacting your GP practice on this occasion?" (% very or fairly good). This is about the last attempt to get in touch, not access in general.

How to read the tables. Every predictor except the training flag is standardised, so each figure is the difference in the outcome (percentage points) associated with being one standard deviation higher on that predictor; the training flag is the difference associated with hosting at least a quarter of a full-time GP registrar. "Alone" looks at one predictor at a time. "Together" puts the operational measures and the practice's circumstances in one model. "+ continuity" adds what patients report about seeing a preferred clinician. Figures in grey are not statistically distinguishable from zero (p≥0.05).

Reading these tables

The two strongest predictors in these tables are both things a practice does. The strongest is continuity — whether patients who have a preferred clinician get to see them when they ask — which is substantially a matter of how appointments are allocated, not a fixed circumstance. The second is answering the phone reliably, which matters most for how patients describe their most recent contact. Where more calls end in the phone menus without reaching the queue, scores are lower — separately from answering. The difference between Monday and Wednesday answering looked important on its own but stops mattering once the overall answer rate is in the model — its apparent effect was the answer rate showing through. Contacts per appointment similarly stops mattering once calls per patient are counted separately, because the two share ingredients. The same-day share is discussed below.

The tables' "circumstances and staffing" block covers the things a practice cannot change quickly: how big it is, how deprived its area is, who its patients are, and how many staff it has. Of these, fully-qualified GPs per patient matters most. Larger practices, practices in more deprived areas, and practices serving more non-white patients all have lower reported scores, whatever they do operationally.

Continuity dominates both models. Whether patients get to see the clinician they prefer (Q7) carries the largest coefficient in either table, and simply having a preferred clinician (Q6) adds its own smaller association. Adding these two variables also changes what the rest of the table means: the size disadvantage largely disappears (fully, for overall experience; by two-thirds, for the most recent contact), most of the online-consultation disadvantage disappears, and the admin-staffing-by-size interaction dissolves. Read cautiously, much of what looked like a cost of being large, or of moving intake online, is the loss of continuity that tends to accompany both — and continuity, unlike size, is something practices can influence through how appointments are allocated when a patient names a clinician.

Training practices score higher — robustly. Practices hosting a GP registrar score about 2.2–2.7 points higher in every combined model, on both questions, over and above all their staffing and circumstances — one of the most stable results in these tables. Point for point of full-time equivalent, trainee GPs are associated with experience much as fully-qualified GPs are. Notably, the training advantage is not carried by continuity — trainees rotate, and trainee staffing goes with slightly lower reported continuity — so training practices score higher despite that, not because of it. The likeliest reading is that training accreditation marks organisational quality the other variables cannot see; the same association was reported from the 2012 survey by Ahluwalia and colleagues (BJGP 2014), when 29% of practices trained. Our flag is cruder: hosting at least a quarter of a full-time registrar in the March 2025 census (59% of practices), which misses accredited practices between placements.

The same-day share might seem counterintuitive. Alone it looks slightly unfavourable; with continuity held constant it turns slightly favourable. Booking models built around same-day access tend to cost continuity; once that cost is accounted for separately, the configuration itself is not the problem.

Admin staffing might seem counterintuitive. Alone, more admin staff goes with higher scores (+0.97). The combined model asks a narrower question: of two practices the same on everything else measured — size, deprivation, GPs, nurses, appointments — does the one with more admin staff score higher? It scores slightly lower (−1.06, or −0.56 with continuity in the model). We tested the obvious explanations. It is not phone workload: adding calls per patient to the model leaves the result unchanged. It is not reception quality: patients at practices with more admin staff rate their reception teams as more helpful, not less (Q4), and allowing for that barely moves it. So we do not know what this remaining association reflects — possibly workload we don't measure, differences in which roles get counted as admin, or the difficulty of dividing credit among staffing numbers that rise and fall together. It should not be read as admin staff lowering scores — and equally, the table gives no support for expecting more admin staff alone to raise them.

Cautions. These are practice-level associations, not causes, and not predictions for any one practice. The continuity variables (Q6, Q7) come from the same survey respondents as the outcomes, so part of their strength reflects the same voices answering both questions; the operational and workforce measures do not share this problem. Contacts per appointment, calls per 1,000 and appointments per 1,000 share ingredients and should be read as a family rather than separately. The training flag and trainee FTE are derived from the difference between all-GP and fully-qualified-GP FTE in the March 2025 census — a placement snapshot, not an accreditation register. Phone measures are from May 2026; survey fieldwork ran January–April 2026. Coefficients in grey have p≥0.05; with this many terms, isolated marginal results should be treated as unconfirmed. The main conclusions replicate in the full population at almost identical size — continuity, GP staffing, the training-practice result, the admin result and the same-day result are not artefacts of which practices have phone data. One instructive difference: practice size is unfavourable again here (−1.12 / −1.90), where in the tables above — with the phone measures included — it was near zero. The reason is that large practices answer fewer of their calls, and this model has no phone variable to carry that fact, so it shows up attached to size instead. Reading the two models together: being large goes with lower scores for two identifiable reasons — patients less often get to see their preferred clinician, and fewer calls get answered. Account for both, and little further disadvantage of size remains.
