# What each measure is for — a plain-English guide for the predictor models

The predictor models exist to find **levers**: things a practice can actually change to give its patients
a better experience. This guide says what job each measure does, so a model treats it the right way — and
so the model audit has something concrete to check against. (Companion to `model-source-audit.md`.)

## The goal — what we're trying to improve

- Overall experience of the practice, experience of getting in touch, and how easy it is to get through on the phone.
- Seeing your own GP (continuity) counts here too — patients value it for its own sake.

## Group 1 — Levers: things a practice can change (this is where to act)

- **The phones:** how many calls get answered; how the phone menu is set up; whether there's a call-back option.
- **The online front door:** how much goes through online requests; whether patients can book a slot themselves.
- **The booking model:** the balance of same-day versus booked-ahead; the mix of in-person, phone and online appointments.
- **Staffing and skill mix** — a slow lever (moves over months and years, not weeks).

In a model: **these are the things whose effect we actually want to measure.** Adjust for the hand a practice is
dealt, but don't let the symptoms below quietly cancel them out.

## Group 2 — Symptoms: what's going wrong (a result, not a switch)

- Being **turned away** / told to get in touch again another day.
- **Couldn't get through** at all.
- **Loss of continuity.**
- Patients **feeling they waited too long.**

These are mostly the **result** of how much capacity a practice has — not switches it can simply flip. So don't
hand them to a practice as "levers to fix." Use them to understand *how* the problem happens. And take care:
dropping a symptom into a model next to a lever can cancel the lever out, because they're links in the same chain.

## Group 3 — The hand you're dealt: adjust for it, never blame it

- Deprivation; how sick the population is (disease registers); the age mix; ethnicity; rurality; list size.

**Always** adjust for these, so practices are compared like with like. **Never** present them as something the
practice should fix.

## Watch-outs — look like they matter, don't

- Same-day booking share on its own; contacts per appointment; appointments per patient.

These don't hold up once everything else is accounted for. Don't build advice on them. At most, show them with a
clear note that they don't actually predict experience.

## Two rules for the whole thing

- **Patterns, not proof.** A single snapshot can show a link — it can't prove that changing something will help.
  The before-and-after evidence (what happened at practices that changed) is what backs a real "do this and it helps."
- **Show the wobble.** A single practice's survey numbers are uncertain (roughly ±10 points). Show that; don't present them as exact.

## What the audit checks, for each model

1. Is the outcome one of the experience measures (the goal), not a symptom dressed up as an outcome?
2. Is every "hand you're dealt" measure adjusted for?
3. For each lever, is its effect isolated **without** a symptom in the same model cancelling it out?
4. Is anything from Group 2 or the watch-outs being presented as a lever? (it shouldn't be)
5. Is each "lever" claim backed by before-and-after evidence, or only a snapshot? (say which)
6. Is the uncertainty shown?
